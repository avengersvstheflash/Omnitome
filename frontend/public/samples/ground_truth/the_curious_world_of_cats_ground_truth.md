## 2. Evaluation Questions, Expected Answers, and Passage References

### Q01 (Factual & Biochemical Causality — Ch. 1)
- **Question:** Why can a single arginine-free meal trigger acute hyperammonemia and potentially fatal neurological crisis in an adult cat within two to five hours, whereas dogs and humans tolerate low-arginine meals?
- **Expected Answer:** First, feline hepatic amino-acid catabolizing enzymes are constitutively active at high baseline rates and cannot downregulate, continuously generating ammonia ($NH_3$) that must be detoxified via the hepatic ornithine-urea cycle. Second, cats express negligible intestinal mucosa activity of *pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase* and *ornithine aminotransferase*, preventing them from synthesizing ornithine and citrulline endogenously from glutamate or proline. Without dietary arginine to replenish hepatic ornithine, the urea cycle stalls immediately and ammonia accumulates in the blood.
- **Reference:** Chapter 1, Section *Hepatic Downregulation Lack and the Biochemical Price of Hypercarnivory* & Table 1.1.

### Q02 (Qualification Preservation — Ch. 1 & Ch. 6)
- **Question:** What exact genetic mutation explains cats' indifference to sucrose, why does *Tas1r3* remain functional, and why do domestic cats still eagerly consume sweet human foods like ice cream?
- **Expected Answer:** A 247-base-pair microdeletion in exon 3 turns the *Tas1r2* gene into an unexpressed pseudogene ($\psi Tas1r2$), preventing assembly of the T1R2/T1R3 sweet-taste receptor heterodimer (Li et al., 2005). *Tas1r3* remains intact and expressed because the T1R3 subunit is required to pair with T1R1 to form the functional umami (amino acid) receptor. Cats eat ice cream or frosting not for sugar, but because their umami and lipid receptors respond to milk fats, short-chain fatty acids, and free amino acids.
- **Reference:** Chapter 1, Section *The Loss of the Sweet Receptor ($Tas1r2$)*; Chapter 6, Section *Bitter Receptors ($Tas2r$) and the Carnivore's Chemical Shield*.

### Q03 (Cross-Chapter Physiological Synthesis — Ch. 1 & Ch. 6)
- **Question:** How does an intact adult male cat's territorial urine marking biochemically compete with his retinal and cardiac health if dietary sulfur amino acids are marginal?
- **Expected Answer:** In Chapter 1, cats are shown to have minimal hepatic *cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase* (CSAD) activity and obligate taurine conjugation of bile acids, making them dependent on dietary taurine/sulfur amino acids (methionine and cysteine) to prevent central retinal degeneration and dilated cardiomyopathy. In Chapter 6 (Deep-Dive 6.1), intact adult males express high levels of the renal enzyme *cauxin*, which cleaves a glutathione/cysteine-derived precursor into up to $95\text{ mg/day}$ of the sulfur-containing amino acid *felinine* (the precursor to volatile pheromone MMB), directly draining the body's cysteine/methionine pool for honest territorial signaling.
- **Reference:** Chapter 1 (*Taurine Conjugation and Retinal/Cardiac Integrity*) and Chapter 6 (*Empirical Deep-Dive 6.1: The Biochemistry of Felinine, Cauxin, and Feline Facial Pheromone Fractions*).

### Q04 (Factual & Paleogenetic Retrieval — Ch. 2)
- **Question:** According to Driscoll et al. (2007) and Ottoni et al. (2017), which wildcat clade gave rise to all modern domestic cats, and what distinguishes the First Wave from the Second Wave of ancient feline dispersal?
- **Expected Answer:** All modern domestic cats cluster within **Clade IV** (*Felis lybica*, the Near Eastern/North African wildcat). The First Wave carried mitochondrial lineage **IV-A** out of Neolithic Anatolia and the Levant into southeastern and central Europe (~8000–4400 BCE) alongside migrating early farmers. The Second Wave carried mitochondrial lineage **IV-C** out of Pharaonic and Greco-Roman Egypt (~1500 BCE – 500 CE) via Mediterranean and Red Sea maritime grain ships, eventually reaching Viking Baltic trading ports such as Ralswiek by the 8th–11th centuries CE.
- **Reference:** Chapter 2, Sections *Resolving the Wildcat Complex: Driscoll et al. (2007)* and *Ancient DNA Reveals Two Waves of Dispersal: Ottoni et al. (2017)*.

### Q05 (Counterfactual & Comparative Reasoning — Ch. 2 & Ch. 3)
- **Question:** Why did the Neolithic commensal relationship with felids at Quanhucun, China (~3500 BCE) fail to produce modern East Asian domestic cats, and why did European wildcats (*Felis silvestris silvestris*) also fail to undergo independent domestication?
- **Expected Answer:** Ancient DNA and morphometrics (Vigne et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2014) revealed that the Quanhucun cats hunting millet-fed rodents were not *Felis lybica*, but Asian leopard cats (*Prionailurus bengalensis*), a separate genus whose commensal partnership ended and was later replaced by Near Eastern *Felis catus* imported along the Silk Road. Similarly, European wildcats (*F. s. silvestris*, Clade I) exhibit persistent defensive hyper-reactivity and poor habituation to humans even when hand-reared from birth, lacking the calmer HPA stress-axis pre-adaptation of *Felis lybica* (Clade IV).
- **Reference:** Chapter 2 (*Empirical Deep-Dive 2.1*) and Chapter 3 (*Comparing Felis lybica and Felis silvestris*).

### Q06 (Temporal & Genetic Qualification — Ch. 3 & Ch. 11)
- **Question:** When did the recessive blotched (classic) tabby coat pattern ($ta^b$) become common in domestic cats, what two-stage molecular mechanism produces it, and what does its late appearance demonstrate about early cat domestication?
- **Expected Answer:** Ancient DNA genotyping by Ottoni et al. (2017) showed that Neolithic, Egyptian, and Roman cats were almost exclusively wild-type mackerel tabbies; the recessive blotched tabby allele ($ta^b$) did not rise to high frequency until the Medieval Period (post-1300 CE in Southwest Asia/Ottoman Empire). Molecularly (Kaelin et al., 2012), loss-of-function mutations in *Transmembrane aminopeptidase Q* (*Taqpep*) expand the fetal skin pre-pattern from narrow stripes into broad whorls, while dermal papilla expression of *Endothelin-3* (*Edn3*) instructs overlying melanocytes to synthesize dark eumelanin. Its late rise proves that for the first ~8,000 years, humans selected cats for pest-control utility and behavioral tameness rather than aesthetic coat mutations.
- **Reference:** Chapter 3 (*Genomic Signatures of Tameness*) and Chapter 11 (*The Molecular Genetics of Coat Color and Pattern*).

### Q07 (Biomechanical Reasoning — Ch. 4)
- **Question:** Why is it anatomically more accurate to call feline claws "protractile" rather than "retractable," and how does the Kane–Scher (1969) model explain mid-air righting without violating conservation of angular momentum?
- **Expected Answer:** At rest, the distal phalanx ($P3$) is held passively folded back inside its sheath by dorsal elastic ligaments without muscular effort; active contraction of both the deep digital flexor and extensor digitorum communis is required to protract (unsheathe) the claw. In the Kane–Scher aerial righting model, total angular momentum remains zero ($\mathbf{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{0}$) because the cat bends its flexible spine at the waist and alternates limb extensions: first tucking the forepaws in (lowering $I_{\text{ant}}$) while extending the hindlimbs (raising $I_{\text{post}}$) to rotate the front half rapidly toward the ground against a small rear counter-rotation, then extending the forelimbs and tucking the hindlimbs to bring the posterior torso into alignment.
- **Reference:** Chapter 4, Sections *The Protractile Claw Mechanism* and *The Physics of the Aerial Righting Reflex*.

### Q08 (Methodological Vulnerability to Over-Compression — Ch. 4)
- **Question:** If a summary states that "cats falling from above the seventh story suffer fewer injuries than cats falling from lower floors," what critical epidemiological bias and physical mechanism have been conflated?
- **Expected Answer:** While a falling cat reaches terminal velocity (~$26.8\text{ m/s}$ or $60\text{ mph}$) around the fifth to seventh story—ceasing acceleration and potentially relaxing limb stiffness—retrospective veterinary clinic data (Whitney & Mehlhaff, 1987) suffer from severe **survivorship bias**: cats that fall from very high floors and die immediately on impact are not brought to veterinary hospitals to be counted in orthopedic injury tallies.
- **Reference:** Chapter 4, Section *The High-Rise Syndrome Paradox and Its Methodological Caveat*.

### Q09 (Spinal Neurophysiology — Ch. 4)
- **Question:** In the feline spinal stumble-corrective reaction (Forssberg et al., 1975), how does the exact same tactile tap on the top (dorsum) of the paw produce opposite muscle activations depending on the phase of the stride?
- **Expected Answer:** The spinal Central Pattern Generator (CPG) gates the reflex by stride phase: if the dorsum of the paw is tapped during the **swing phase**, spinal interneurons fire the limb's **flexor muscles** within 25–35 ms to lift the paw over the obstacle; if tapped during the weight-bearing **stance phase**, the CPG routes the signal to the **extensor muscles** so the leg braces rather than collapsing.
- **Reference:** Chapter 4, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 4.1: Spinal Central Pattern Generators, Cutaneous Stumble Reflexes, and Gait Kinematics*.

### Q10 (Sensory Trade-Offs — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** What optical structures enable *Felis catus* to see in light six to seven times dimmer than humans, and what specific trade-offs in visual acuity, near-focus distance, and color vision result from this adaptation?
- **Expected Answer:** Low-light sensitivity is driven by a wide cornea, a 135-fold dynamic-range vertical slit pupil, a high rod-to-cone photoreceptor ratio (~10:1 in the *area centralis* and $>25:1$ peripherally) with spatial summation, and a *tapetum lucidum cellulosum* packed with crystalline riboflavin and zinc-cysteine rods. The trade-offs are reduced spatial visual acuity (~20/100 to 20/200 vs. human 20/20), inability to focus optically on objects closer than 25–30 cm in front of the snout (compensated by mystacial and carpal vibrissae), and dichromatic color vision peaking near 450 nm (blue-violet) and 554 nm (greenish-yellow) without red-cone separation.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Section *Crepuscular Optics: The Tapetum Lucidum, Pupil Geometry, and Color Trade-Offs*.

### Q11 (Acoustic & Anatomical Integration — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** How does a small-skulled animal like *Felis catus* simultaneously achieve low-frequency hearing down to 48 Hz and ultrasonic localization up to 85,000 Hz?
- **Expected Answer:** Low-frequency sensitivity down to 48 Hz is enabled by the enlarged bony **auditory bulla**, which is divided by an incomplete septum into two coupled acoustic chambers (tympanic and endotympanic cavities) that act as a Helmholtz resonator increasing tympanic membrane compliance. High-frequency sensitivity up to 85 kHz (tuned to 22–70 kHz rodent ultrasonic calls) relies on a basilar membrane tuned to ultrasonic frequencies and 32-muscle mobile pinnae whose cartilage ridges generate direction-dependent spectral notches between 10 kHz and 40 kHz for $5^\circ$ azimuth and vertical elevation pinpointing.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Section *Ultrasonic Localization and the Pinna Satellite Dish* & Figure A.2.

### Q12 (Neurobiology of Vision — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** How do X and Y retinal ganglion cells divide labor in the feline retina, and what property distinguishes cortical simple/complex cells in Area 17 (V1) from retinal ganglion cells?
- **Expected Answer:** X (beta) cells are concentrated in the *area centralis* with small receptive fields and linear spatial summation for sustained high-acuity inspection of stationary forms, whereas giant Y (alpha) cells tile the retina with large dendritic arbors, fast-conducting axons ($30\text{–}40\text{ m/s}$), and nonlinear transient bursts that detect rapid peripheral motion. In V1 (Area 17), Hubel and Wiesel showed that cortical simple and complex cells respond selectively to **oriented bars/edges** moving in specific directions rather than the circular center-surround spots of retinal and dLGN neurons.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 5.1: Retinal Ganglion Cell Channels (X, Y, W) and Cortical Feature Detection in V1*.

### Q13 (Chemosensory Neuroanatomy — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** Contrast the physical stimulus type, anatomical entry pathway, and forebrain target projections of the feline Main Olfactory Epithelium (MOE) versus the Vomeronasal Organ (VNO).
- **Expected Answer:** The MOE ($~20\text{ cm}^2$, 60–200 million receptor neurons) detects volatile airborne odorants inhaled through the nasal passages and projects via the main olfactory bulb to the piriform cortex for cognitive odor recognition and food evaluation. The VNO (Jacobson's organ) detects heavy, non-volatile fluid-phase pheromones and urinary proteins pumped in saliva through the **incisive papilla and nasopalatine ducts** behind the upper incisors during the **Flehmen response**, projecting via the accessory olfactory bulb directly to the **medial amygdala and ventromedial hypothalamus**.
- **Reference:** Chapter 6, Section *The Main Olfactory System Versus the Vomeronasal (Jacobson's) Organ*.

### Q14 (Evolutionary Paradox — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** Why does an obligate carnivore that eats almost no plants retain twelve functional *Tas2r* bitter taste receptor genes, and why do cats prefer food warmed to $35^\circ\text{C}–38^\circ\text{C}$?
- **Expected Answer:** Whole vertebrate and invertebrate prey consumed by cats (amphibians, reptiles, insects, and rodent gastrointestinal tracts) often contain toxic bitter alkaloids, cardiotonic steroids, and bile salts, and putrefying carrion generates bitter microbial amines; thus functional *Tas2r* receptors act as a poison shield. Cats prefer food at $35^\circ\text{C}–38^\circ\text{C}$ because this matches the core body temperature of freshly killed mammalian prey, optimizing volatile meat aroma release and thermal activation of TRPM5 ion channels in taste receptor cells.
- **Reference:** Chapter 6, Section *Bitter Receptors ($Tas2r$) and the Carnivore's Chemical Shield*.

### Q15 (Ethological & Conservation Synthesis — Ch. 7)
- **Question:** Why do well-fed indoor-outdoor pet cats continue to stalk and kill wildlife they do not eat, what percentage of kills do they actually bring home, and what two non-lethal interventions did Cecchetti et al. (2021) prove significantly reduce predation?
- **Expected Answer:** Because a wildcat requires 8–12 mice per day and most stalks fail, evolution decoupled the neural drive for the appetitive predatory phases (stalking, chasing, pouncing, killing) from hypothalamic gastric satiety. Video-collar data (Loyd et al., 2013) show cats bring home only **~23%** of the prey they kill (consuming ~28% in the field and leaving ~49% at the capture site). Cecchetti et al. (2021) proved in a randomized trial that feeding a high-meat protein diet reduced prey brought home by **36%**, 5–10 minutes of daily interactive object play reduced captures by **25%**, and colorful "Birdsbesafe" collar bibs reduced bird captures by **42%**.
- **Reference:** Chapter 7, Sections *The Predatory Motor Sequence*, *The Ecological Toll of Free-Ranging Cats*, and *Empirical Deep-Dive 7.1*.

### Q16 (Biomechanics & Epistemic Boundary — Ch. 8)
- **Question:** How did Herbst et al. (2023) challenge the classical 1972 neural oscillator model of feline purring, and what two explicit scientific qualifications must be preserved when summarizing purring research?
- **Expected Answer:** Herbst et al. (2023) passed warm, humidified air through eight excised domestic cat larynges completely severed from neural input and demonstrated self-sustained purring oscillations at 25–30 Hz driven by the passive **myoelastic-aerodynamic (MEAD)** mechanism and specialized 4 mm fibro-elastic pads inside the vocal folds. Qualification 1: Excised-larynx sufficiency proves cyclical 25 Hz neural muscle twitching is not strictly required, but does not rule out descending neural regulation of glottal tension in living cats. Qualification 2: The popular claim that cats purr at 25–50 Hz to heal their own bones remains an unvalidated evolutionary hypothesis with zero controlled clinical fracture-healing trials in cats.
- **Reference:** Chapter 8, Section *The Biomechanics of Purring: Neural Oscillators Versus Myoelastic-Aerodynamic Self-Oscillation*.

### Q17 (Bioacoustics & Semiotics — Ch. 8)
- **Question:** What acoustic feature distinguishes a cat's food-solicitation purr from a contented resting purr (McComb et al., 2009), and what distinguishes an affiliative tail posture from an agitated tail movement?
- **Expected Answer:** Food-solicitation purrs contain an embedded high-frequency voiced cry/mew peak between **300 Hz and 600 Hz** (mean ~380–450 Hz, matching the fundamental frequency of a human infant's distress cry) superimposed on the 25 Hz purr. Visually, a vertical **tail-up** ($90^\circ$) posture signals friendly, non-aggressive greeting, whereas lateral tail lashing or thumping signals high autonomic arousal, frustration, or impending defensive/predatory action (the opposite of a dog's friendly tail wag).
- **Reference:** Chapter 8, Section *"The Cry Embedded Within the Purr": McComb et al. (2009)* & Table 8.1.

### Q18 (Neurophysiology & Bioenergetics — Ch. 9)
- **Question:** What brainstem mechanism prevents a sleeping cat from acting out its dreams during REM sleep, and why does a cat in a $20^\circ\text{C}$ ($68^\circ\text{F}$) living room seek out small cardboard boxes?
- **Expected Answer:** As Michel Jouvet demonstrated, inhibitory nuclei in the pontine tegmentum (*locus coeruleus alpha*) hyperpolarize spinal motor neurons during REM (paradoxical) sleep to enforce postural muscle atonia. Bioenergetically, the feline thermoneutral zone is **$30^\circ\text{C}\text{ to }38^\circ\text{C}$** ($86^\circ\text{F}–100.4^\circ\text{F}$); a $20^\circ\text{C}$ room is $10^\circ\text{C}$ below its lower critical temperature. Curling inside a corrugated cardboard box (thermal conductivity $\approx 0.045\text{–}0.06\text{ W/m}\cdot\text{K}$) traps radiated body heat to warm the local microclimate while simultaneously lowering behavioral stress scores (Vinke et al., 2014).
- **Reference:** Chapter 9, Sections *Polyphasic Sleep Architecture* and *Empirical Deep-Dive 9.1*.

### Q19 (Social Ecology & Endocrine Stress — Ch. 9 & Ch. 10)
- **Question:** Under what ecological condition do free-ranging female cats transition from solitary territoriality to matrilineal colonies, and why do fecal glucocorticoid metabolite (FGM) studies show that the quietest cat in a multi-cat apartment often suffers the highest chronic stress?
- **Expected Answer:** Under the Resource Dispersion Hypothesis, when food is abundant and clumped (e.g., farm barns, dumpsters, feeding stations), related queens form matrilineal colonies that allonurse, allogroom, and allorub, while remaining xenophobic toward outsiders. In restricted apartments, inter-cat conflict is often passive and silent: a high-Dominance cat uses unblinking stares and hallway blocking to restrict a high-Neuroticism subordinate cat's access to litter boxes and food without overt fighting, elevating the silent subordinate's HPA cortisol output and risking Feline Idiopathic Cystitis (FIC).
- **Reference:** Chapter 10, Sections *The Resource Dispersion Hypothesis and Matrilineal Colonies* and *Empirical Deep-Dive 10.1*.

### Q20 (Attachment & Social Cognition — Ch. 10)
- **Question:** What were the quantitative results of the Vitale et al. (2019) Secure Base Test in kittens and adult cats, and how do cats differ from dogs when confronted with an unsolvable food-container task (Miklósi et al., 2005)?
- **Expected Answer:** In Vitale et al. (2019), **64.3% of kittens** and **65.8% of adult cats** were securely attached to their human caregivers (using the human as a secure base to restore exploration after separation), while ~34–36% were insecurely attached (among whom 84% were insecure-ambivalent, 12% insecure-avoidant, and 4% disorganized). In Miklósi et al. (2005), cats followed human pointing gestures as accurately as dogs, but when faced with a locked unsolvable food container, dogs quickly looked back at the human's face for help whereas cats persisted in trying to open the container themselves.
- **Reference:** Chapter 10, Sections *Attachment Bonds to Humans: Vitale et al. (2019)* and *Feline Social and Physical Cognition*.

### Q21 (Ontogeny & Gene-by-Environment Interaction — Ch. 11)
- **Question:** What is the exact age window of the primary sensitive period for kitten socialization (Karsh, 1988), and how did McCune (1995) prove that paternal genetics independently influence adult boldness?
- **Expected Answer:** The primary socialization window opens at **2 weeks of age and closes around 7 weeks of age** (with diminishing plasticity up to weeks 9–12). Because tomcats play zero role in kitten rearing, McCune (1995) bred queens to either a bold/friendly sire or a timid sire and raised half of each litter with or without early handling (Weeks 2–12). Kittens sired by the friendly father were significantly bolder toward novel people and objects at 1 year of age even without ever meeting him, and kittens receiving *both* a bold paternal genotype and early handling showed the highest adult confidence.
- **Reference:** Chapter 11, Sections *Ontogeny and the Sensitive Period for Socialization* and *Empirical Deep-Dive 11.1*.

### Q22 (Coat Genetics & Pleiotropy — Ch. 11)
- **Question:** Explain the genetic mechanisms behind (a) why almost all tortoiseshell/calico cats are female, (b) why dominant-white ($W$) blue-eyed cats are frequently deaf, and (c) why Siamese kittens are born completely white and develop dark extremities later.
- **Expected Answer:** (a) The Orange ($O$) locus is on the X chromosome; females have two X chromosomes ($X^O X^o$) and undergo random embryonic X-inactivation (**Lyonization**) into Barr bodies, creating mosaic orange and black skin patches (whereas male calicos require rare $XXY$ Klinefelter-like aneuploidy or chimerism and are typically sterile). (b) Dominant White ($W$, a full *FERV1* insertion in *KIT*) blocks neural-crest melanoblast survival; because melanocytes in the **stria vascularis** of the inner ear cochlea are required to maintain the endocochlear potential, 65%–85% of two-blue-eyed dominant-white cats suffer congenital sensorineural deafness. (c) Siamese cats carry a temperature-sensitive missense mutation ($c^s$) in the **tyrosinase ($TYR$)** gene; the enzyme is inactive at uterine core body temperature ($38.5^\circ\text{C}$) and folds into an active melanin-synthesizing state only on cooler extremities ($<33^\circ\text{C}$).
- **Reference:** Chapter 11, Section *The Molecular Genetics of Coat Color and Pattern*.

### Q23 (Clinical Welfare & Skeletal Pathology — Ch. 12)
- **Question:** What gene mutation causes the folded ears of the Scottish Fold breed, why does it create an inescapable welfare problem, and what anatomical amputation occurs during surgical declawing (onychectomy)?
- **Expected Answer:** Scottish Fold ears are caused by an autosomal dominant missense mutation in the **$TRPV4$ calcium channel gene**, which disrupts cartilage and bone maturation across the entire skeleton and causes progressive, painful **osteochondrodysplasia** of the limbs and tail in folded-ear cats. Surgical declawing (onychectomy) is the amputation of the entire **distal phalanx ($P3$)** of each toe at the distal interphalangeal joint, severing tendons, nerves, and dorsal elastic ligaments and increasing the odds of chronic neuropathic back pain, litter-box aversion, and biting.
- **Reference:** Chapter 12, Section *Welfare Controversies: Extreme Conformations and Declawing (Onychectomy)*.

### Q24 (Environmental Design — Ch. 12)
- **Question:** What are the Five Pillars of a healthy feline environment defined by the 2013 AAFP/ISFM Guidelines (Ellis et al., 2013), and how does the $N+1$ rule apply to a three-cat household?
- **Expected Answer:** The Five Pillars are: (1) provide a safe, elevated hiding/resting place; (2) provide multiple and spatially separated key resources (food, water, toileting, scratching, resting); (3) provide opportunity for play and predatory behavior; (4) provide positive, consistent, predictable human–cat social interaction with high feline agency; and (5) provide an environment that respects the cat's sense of smell and other senses. In a three-cat ($N=3$) household, the $N+1$ rule requires at least **4 litter boxes** (and multiple separated water/feeding/perch stations) placed in **four distinct physical locations** out of visual line-of-sight rather than side-by-side.
- **Reference:** Chapter 12, Section *The Five Pillars of a Healthy Feline Environment: Ellis et al. (2013)*.

### Q25 (Cross-Chapter Synthesis: Neuroendocrinology & Population Ecology — Ch. 12)
- **Question:** What dual neurobiological and ecological function did Uenoyama et al. (2021) discover for catnip/silver vine rubbing, and why do low-intensity Trap-Neuter-Return (TNR) programs that sterilize only 30% of a feral colony fail to reduce colony size?
- **Expected Answer:** Uenoyama et al. (2021) demonstrated that facial rubbing against **nepetalactone** (catnip) and **nepetalactol** (silver vine) activates the **$\mu$-opioid system** (elevating plasma $\beta$-endorphin) while transferring plant iridoids onto the fur that chemically repel *Aedes* mosquitoes. Ecologically, matrix population models (Andersen et al., 2004; Boone et al., 2019) show that because fertile queens produce 4–12 surviving kittens per year, sterilizing only 30% of a colony is offset by compensatory kitten survival and immigration; sustained population decline requires sterilizing **$71\%\text{ to }80\%$ or more** of fertile adults while preventing new abandonment.
- **Reference:** Chapter 12, Sections *Unresolved Questions in Feline Science* and *Empirical Deep-Dive 12.1*.
