## 2. Evaluation Questions, Expected Answers, and Passage References

### Q01 (Empirical vs. True Risk & Bayes Estimands — Ch. 1)
- **Question:** Define True Risk $R(f_\theta)$ and Empirical Risk $\hat{R}_{\text{emp}}(f_\theta)$, prove which point estimands minimize expected squared error vs. expected absolute error, and calculate the Hoeffding $50\%$-chance false-discovery threshold $\epsilon$ when picking the best of $M = 1,000$ random coin-flip classifiers on $n_{\text{val}} = 50$ rows.
- **Expected Answer:** True Risk $R(f_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{X},Y)\sim P}[L(Y, f_\theta(\mathbf{X}))]$ is the expected loss over the unknown data-generating distribution $P$, whereas Empirical Risk $\hat{R}_{\text{emp}}(f_\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n L(y_i, f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i))$ is the finite training sample average. Differentiating under the integral sign shows that minimizing expected squared error $\mathbb{E}[(Y-c)^2\mid\mathbf{x}]$ yields the **conditional mean** $c^* = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathbf{x}]$, whereas minimizing expected absolute error $\mathbb{E}[|Y-c|\mid\mathbf{x}]$ yields the **conditional median** ($P(Y\le c^*\mid\mathbf{x}) = 0.50$). By Hoeffding's inequality and the union bound, setting $1000\,e^{-2(50)\epsilon^2} = 0.50 \implies \epsilon = \sqrt{-\ln(0.0005)/100} \approx \mathbf{0.2757}$, meaning the best of $1,000$ pure noise models achieves $50\% - 27.6\% = \mathbf{22.4\%}$ validation error ($77.6\%$ accuracy) half the time!
- **Reference:** Chapter 1, Sections *The Mathematical Objective* and *1.C Chapter 1 Exercises and Worked Solutions*.

### Q02 (Probabilistic-Geometric Duality of Loss Functions — Ch. 2)
- **Question:** Which noise distributions under Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) correspond to (a) Mean Squared Error and (b) Mean Absolute Error, and which Bayesian weight priors under MAP estimation correspond to (c) Ridge ($L_2$) and (d) Lasso ($L_1$) regularization?
- **Expected Answer:** (a) Independent **Gaussian noise** $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ has negative log-likelihood proportional to $\sum (y_i - f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i))^2$ (**MSE**). (b) Independent **Laplace (double-exponential) noise** $\epsilon \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b)$ has negative log-likelihood proportional to $\sum |y_i - f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)|$ (**MAE**). Under MAP estimation with Gaussian noise, (c) a zero-mean **Gaussian prior** on weights $\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$ yields the quadratic **Ridge ($L_2$)** penalty $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$, whereas (d) a zero-mean **Laplace prior** $p(\beta_j) \propto \exp(-|\beta_j|/b)$ yields the absolute-value **Lasso ($L_1$)** penalty.
- **Reference:** Chapter 2, Section *2.D Mathematical Deep-Dive: Equivalence of Maximum Likelihood and Empirical Risk Loss Functions*.

### Q03 (Missing Data Mechanisms & Prior Probability Shift — Ch. 3)
- **Question:** Contrast MCAR, MAR, and MNAR missingness (Rubin, 1976), explain why a binary missingness indicator column $m_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ is required alongside median imputation, and calculate the recalibrated deployment probability $\hat{p}_{\text{deploy}}(\mathbf{x})$ when a classifier trained at base rate $\pi_{\text{train}} = 0.05$ outputs $\hat{p}_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{x}) = 0.40$ in a deployment environment where base rate drops to $\pi_{\text{deploy}} = 0.01$.
- **Expected Answer:** **MCAR:** missingness is independent of all variables; **MAR:** missingness depends on other *observed* features (e.g., idle load); **MNAR:** missingness depends on the *unobserved value itself* (e.g., sensor saturation above $120^\circ\text{C}$). Under MAR/MNAR, median imputation alone erases the informative signal that the sensor was missing, requiring a binary indicator $m_{ij} = \mathbb{I}(x_{ij}\text{ is NaN})$. Under pure prior shift ($\pi_{\text{train}}=0.05 \to \pi_{\text{deploy}}=0.01$), Bayes' rule scales the posterior odds by the prior-odds ratio: $\text{Odds}_{\text{deploy}} = \left(\frac{0.40}{0.60}\cdot\frac{0.95}{0.05}\right)\frac{0.01}{0.99} = 0.12795 \implies \hat{p}_{\text{deploy}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{0.12795}{1.12795} \approx \mathbf{0.1134}$ ($11.34\%$).
- **Reference:** Chapter 3, Sections *Missing Data Mechanisms*, *Dataset Shift*, and *Exercise 3.1*.

### Q05 (High-Dimensional Feature Selection Bleed & Target Encoding — Ch. 4)
- **Question:** Why does selecting the top 10 correlated features from $p = 5,000$ random columns across $n = 50$ rows *before* running 5-fold CV fabricate ~90% CV accuracy on pure coin-flip noise (Varma & Simon, 2006), and what two safeguards are required to prevent self-leakage in Target Encoding?
- **Expected Answer:** With $n = 50$ rows, the standard error of sample correlation under the null is $\approx 1/\sqrt{48} \approx 0.144$; across $5,000$ random columns, extreme-value statistics guarantee 10 columns will have spurious correlations $|r_j| \approx 0.45\text{–}0.55$ with $\mathbf{y}$ across *all 50 rows* (including the validation folds!), contaminating every CV fold. In Target Encoding, computing category means $\bar{y}_g$ directly on the training set leaks row $i$'s own target $y_i$ into its feature value (especially for rare categories); preventing this requires (1) **Out-of-Fold (OOF) internal target encoding** so $y_i$ is excluded from its own category mean, and (2) **Empirical Bayes additive smoothing** $\hat{\mu}_g^{\text{smooth}} = \frac{n_g\bar{y}_g + m\bar{y}_{\text{train}}}{n_g + m}$ shrinking rare categories toward the global prior.
- **Reference:** Chapter 4, Sections *4.A Worked Proof & Simulation* and *4.D Case Study in Subtle Leakage*.

### Q06 (Cross-Validation Topologies & Purge Gaps — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** State the mathematical disjointness invariant of `GroupKFold`, calculate the expected validation MSE gap between Random 5-Fold CV and Group 5-Fold CV in Exercise 5.1 ($\text{Var}(u_g)=16.0, \sigma_\epsilon^2=4.0$), and explain why a purge gap $h_{\text{gap}} \ge h$ is required in rolling-origin time evaluation when predicting an $h$-hour forward horizon.
- **Expected Answer:** `GroupKFold` enforces $\{g_i : i \in \mathcal{F}_k\} \cap \{g_j : j \in \mathcal{F}_{k'}\} = \emptyset$ for all $k \neq k'$, keeping all rows of any physical `unit_id` together. In Exercise 5.1, Random Row-Level 5-Fold CV allows the model to memorize unit offsets $\hat{u}_g \approx u_g$, reporting an optimistic validation MSE of $\sigma_\epsilon^2 = \mathbf{4.0\;(^\circ\text{C})^2}$, whereas true generalization MSE on an unseen 31st unit (measured honestly by Group 5-Fold CV) is $\text{Var}(u_g) + \sigma_\epsilon^2 = 16.0 + 4.0 = \mathbf{20.0\;(^\circ\text{C})^2}$! In rolling-origin time splits, if the last training decision timestamp $\tau_k$ predicts a forward horizon $[\tau_k+1, \tau_k+h]$, its label $y_{\tau_k}$ observes outcomes up to $\tau_k+h$; starting validation before $\tau_k + h + 1$ leaks the outcome of those $h$ hours into training, requiring a purged buffer of $h_{\text{gap}} \ge h$ timestamps.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Sections *Choosing the Right Cross-Validation Topology*, *5.A Worked Calculation*, and *Exercise 5.1*.

### Q07 (Bias-Variance Trade-Off of $K$-Fold Estimators — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** Compare $K = 2$, $K = 5\text{ or }10$, and Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation ($K = n$) in terms of estimator bias, estimator variance, and computational cost.
- **Expected Answer:** **$K = 2$** trains on only $50\%$ of the data ($n/2$), incurring significant **pessimistic (upward) bias** as an estimator of $R_n$. **LOOCV ($K = n$)** trains on $n-1$ rows (near-zero bias), but requires $n$ model fits and suffers from **higher estimator variance** on unstable learners because the $n$ training sets share $n-2$ identical rows. **$K = 5$ or $K = 10$** trains on $80\%\text{–}90\%$ of the data, balancing low pessimistic bias with moderate variance and $5\text{–}10\times$ compute cost.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Section *5.D Mathematical Proof: Unbiasedness and Variance of K-Fold Cross-Validation Risk Estimators*.

### Q08 (OLS vs. Ridge Linear Algebra & Executed Turbine Coefficients — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** Write the closed-form matrix equations for OLS and Ridge regression (including how the intercept $\beta_0$ is handled in $\mathbf{I}_0$), explain via eigenvalues why collinearity inflates OLS variance, and report the exact executed Test RMSE and $R^2$ for OLS, Ridge ($\lambda = 8.5317$), and Lasso ($\lambda = 0.2500$) from Table 6.1.

### Q09 (Worked Collinear $2 \times 2$ Ridge Calculation & Hoerl-Kennard Theorem — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** In the worked $2 \times 2$ collinear Gram matrix example of Section 6.A ($r_{12} = 0.99$, $\mathbf{c} = [2.00, 1.96]^\top$), what are the exact OLS vs. Ridge ($\lambda = 0.10$) coefficient estimates, and why does the Hoerl-Kennard (1970) theorem guarantee that some $\lambda^* > 0$ always beats OLS in expected MSE?
- **Expected Answer:** For **OLS ($\lambda=0$)**, $\det(\mathbf{G}) = 0.0199$ ($\text{VIF} = 50.25$), yielding unstable opposite-sign weights **$\hat{\beta}_1 = +2.9950, \hat{\beta}_2 = -1.0050$**. Adding **Ridge ($\lambda = 0.10$)** raises the determinant to $\det(\mathbf{G}_{\text{Ridge}}) = 0.2299$ and stabilizes the weights to **$\hat{\beta}_1 = +1.1292, \hat{\beta}_2 = +0.7656$** while cutting variance by $>90\%$. ### Q10 (Lasso Sparsity Geometry & Soft-Thresholding — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** What two mathematical properties of the $L_1$ penalty $\lambda\sum |\beta_j|$ allow Lasso (Tibshirani, 1996) to set coefficients to exact zero, and what are the soft-thresholding updates $S(\rho_j, \lambda)$ for $\rho_j = -0.42$ at $\lambda = 0.15$ and $\lambda = 0.50$?
- **Expected Answer:** Geometrically, the $L_1$ constraint region $\sum |\beta_j| \le t$ is a sharp **cross-polytope (diamond)** whose vertices lie on the coordinate axes where $\beta_j = 0$ (unlike Ridge's smooth hypersphere). Analytically, the subgradient of $|\beta_j|$ maintains constant magnitude $\pm\lambda$ down to $0$, yielding the coordinate-descent **soft-thresholding operator** $S(\rho_j, \lambda) = \text{sign}(\rho_j)\max(0, |\rho_j| - \lambda)$. For $\rho_j = -0.42$: at $\lambda = 0.15$, $S(-0.42, 0.15) = \mathbf{-0.27}$; at $\lambda = 0.50$, since $|-0.42| \le 0.50$, $S(-0.42, 0.50) = \mathbf{0.0000}$.
- **Reference:** Chapter 6, Sections *Lasso ($L_1$ Regularization)* and *Exercise 6.1*.

### Q12 (IRLS Optimization, Complete Separation & Softmax Stability — Ch. 7)
- **Question:** Write the diagonal weighting matrix $\mathbf{W}^{(t)}$ and Hessian $\mathbf{H}^{(t)}$ for binary logistic regression IRLS, explain why unregularized logistic weights diverge under complete linear separation, and show how the Log-Sum-Exp trick prevents numerical overflow in Softmax.
- **Expected Answer:** In IRLS, $\mathbf{W}^{(t)} = \text{diag}\big(\hat{p}_i^{(t)}(1 - \hat{p}_i^{(t)})\big)$ and $\mathbf{H}^{(t)} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{W}^{(t)}\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_0$. Under complete linear separation (where a hyperplane separates all $y_i=1$ from $y_i=0$), unregularized MLE drives $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \to \pm\infty$ to push $\sigma(z_i) \to 1.0$, requiring $\lambda > 0$ ($L_2$ penalty) to guarantee a finite minimum. In Softmax, subtracting $M_i = \max_c z_{ic}$ from every exponent ($\frac{\exp(z_{ik}-M_i)}{\sum_c \exp(z_{ic}-M_i)}$) leaves the probability ratio algebraically identical while bounding all exponents $\le 0$, preventing `exp()` overflow above $+710$.
- **Reference:** Chapter 7, Sections *7.B Field Troubleshooting Notes* and *7.D Mathematical Deep-Dive*.

### Q14 (Random Forests vs. Gradient Boosting & Second-Order Taylor Splits — Ch. 8)
- **Question:** Using the ensemble variance equation $\text{Var}(\bar{T}) = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$, explain how Breiman's (2001) Random Forests improve upon Bagging, prove why Out-of-Bag (OOB) samples cover $\approx 36.8\%$ of training data, and state the optimal leaf weight $w_j^*$ in second-order Newton boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM).
- **Expected Answer:** Pure Bagging reduces only the second term $\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$ while leaving pairwise tree correlation $\rho$ high because all trees split on the same dominant features at the root; Random Forests restrict each split to $m_{\text{try}} < p$ random features, reducing $\rho$ (in Exercise 8.2, dropping $\rho$ from $0.65$ to $0.25$ at $B=100, \sigma^2=12$ cuts ensemble variance from $7.842$ to $3.090$). ### Q16 (Clustering Geometry: $K$-Means vs. DBSCAN, GMMs & Isolation Forests — Ch. 9)
- **Question:** What are the three geometric failure modes of $K$-Means, how do GMMs and DBSCAN overcome them, and how does an Isolation Forest detect anomalies without estimating high-dimensional density?
- **Expected Answer:** $K$-Means fails because (1) inertia $J_{\text{WCSS}}$ decreases monotonically with $K$ (requiring Silhouette scores $s(i) = \frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max(a(i),b(i))}$), (2) Euclidean distance assumes isotropic spherical clusters of equal radius, and (3) sample means are sensitive to outliers. **GMMs** fit ellipsoidal clusters with full covariance matrices $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$ via EM; **DBSCAN** connects core points ($\ge \text{MinPts}$ within radius $\varepsilon$) into arbitrary non-convex manifolds while labeling isolated points as noise (`-1`). **Isolation Forests** recursively partition random features at uniform random split values; anomalies sitting in sparse regions are isolated into single-point leaves in significantly **fewer splits (shorter average path length)** than normal points.
- **Reference:** Chapter 9, Sections *$K$-Means Clustering*, *9.C Exercises*, and *9.D Algorithmic Deep-Dive*.

### Q17 (Regression Metrics, Outlier Ratios & $R^2$ Pathologies — Ch. 10)

### Q18 (Log-Target Smearing, Quantile Pinball Loss & Conformal Prediction — Ch. 10)
- **Question:** Why does $\hat{y} = \exp(\hat{z})$ underestimate the conditional mean when regressing on $z = \ln(y)$, how does the Pinball Loss $\rho_{\tau_q}(e)$ weight errors for the $95\text{th}$ percentile ($\tau_q = 0.95$), and how does Split Conformal Prediction guarantee $1-\alpha$ coverage?
- **Expected Answer:** Because $\exp(z)$ is strictly convex, Jensen's inequality implies $\mathbb{E}[e^Z] > e^{\mathbb{E}[Z]}$; for Gaussian residuals $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)$, unbiased conditional mean recovery requires multiplying by the log-normal correction factor $\exp(\hat{z}_i + \frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_\epsilon^2)$. For $\tau_q = 0.95$, the Pinball Loss $\rho_{0.95}(e_i) = \max(0.95 e_i, -0.05 e_i)$ penalizes under-predictions ($y_i > \hat{q}$) **$19\times$ more heavily** than over-predictions. **Split Conformal Prediction** computes absolute residuals $s_i = |y_i - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_i)|$ on a held-out calibration set of size $n_{\text{cal}}$ and sets the interval half-width $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}$ to the $\lceil(n_{\text{cal}}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest calibration residual, guaranteeing $P(Y_{\text{test}} \in [\hat{f} \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}]) \ge 1-\alpha$ under exchangeability without parametric assumptions.

### Q19 (Imbalanced Classification: ROC-AUC vs. PR-AUC & Rare-Event Math — Ch. 11)
- **Question:** Explain mathematically why $\text{ROC-AUC}$ is optimistic under severe class imbalance (Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015), prove why a detector with $99.0\%$ sensitivity and $99.0\%$ specificity yields only **$9.02\%$ Precision** at a $0.1\%$ fault base rate, and state the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney ranking identity of $\text{ROC-AUC}$.
- **Expected Answer:** In the ROC curve, False Positives ($\text{FP}$) are divided by the huge majority-class denominator $\text{FP} + \text{TN}$ in $\text{FPR} = \frac{\text{FP}}{\text{FP}+\text{TN}}$, keeping $\text{FPR}$ tiny even when $\text{FP}$ equals or exceeds $\text{TP}$; in the Precision-Recall curve, $\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}}$ compares $\text{FP}$ directly against $\text{TP}$. At a $0.1\%$ base rate across $100,000$ hours ($100$ faults, $99,900$ normal), $99\%$ sensitivity catches $\text{TP} = 99$ faults ($\text{FN}=1$), while $99\%$ specificity ($1\%$ FPR) generates $\text{FP} = 999$ false alarms ($\text{TN}=98,901$), yielding $\text{Precision} = \frac{99}{99 + 999} = \mathbf{9.02\%}$ ($90.98\%$ false alarms) and $F_1 = \mathbf{0.1653}$. Mathematically, $\text{ROC-AUC} = P(\hat{p}(\mathbf{X}_+) > \hat{p}(\mathbf{X}_-))$ (the normalized Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney $U$ statistic across all $n_+ n_-$ pairs), which is purely rank-based and blind to probability calibration.
- **Reference:** Chapter 11, Sections *Why Precision-Recall Curves Beat ROC Curves*, *11.A Worked Calculation*, and *11.D Mathematical Deep-Dive*.

### Q23 (Fairness Impossibility Theorem & Subgroup Auditing — Ch. 13)
- **Question:** State Chouldechova's (2017) impossibility theorem regarding algorithmic fairness metrics, and explain how aggregation bias can hide subgroup failures behind a high aggregate score.
- **Expected Answer:** Chouldechova (2017) proved that whenever true base rates $\pi_A \neq \pi_B$ differ across two subgroups and a classifier is not $100\%$ error-free, **it is mathematically impossible to simultaneously satisfy probability calibration within both groups AND Equalized Odds (equal False Positive Rates and equal False Negative Rates across both groups)**.
