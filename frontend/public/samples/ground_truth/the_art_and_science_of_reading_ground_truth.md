## 2. Evaluation Questions, Expected Answers, and Passage References

### Q01 (Neurobiology & Evolutionary Constraints — Ch. 1)
- **Question:** Why could natural selection not have evolved a dedicated genetic module for reading, what cortical region is recycled during literacy acquisition (Dehaene, 2009), and what two pre-existing properties make that specific brain patch suitable for reading?
- **Expected Answer:** Writing was invented only ~5,200–5,400 years ago (~3400–3200 BCE, fewer than 200 human generations), far too recently for genetic evolution of a dedicated reading organ. Instead, literacy recycles the left **Visual Word Form Area (VWFA)** in the lateral occipitotemporal sulcus ($x \approx -42, y \approx -57, z \approx -12$). That patch is uniquely suited because (1) its neurons are pre-tuned in primates to high-resolution foveal **line junctions** (`T`, `L`, `Y` vertices), and (2) it possesses pre-existing direct white-matter fiber tracts (arcuate, inferior longitudinal, and vertical occipital fasciculi) connecting it to left-hemisphere phonological (Wernicke's) and articulatory/syntactic (Broca's) language areas even before a child learns the alphabet.
- **Reference:** Chapter 1, Section *Stanislas Dehaene and the Neuronal Recycling Hypothesis*.

### Q02 (Cross-Linguistic Qualification — Ch. 1)
- **Question:** According to Psycholinguistic Grain Size Theory (Ziegler & Goswami, 2005) and cross-national studies (Seymour et al., 2003; Paulesu et al., 2001), how does the behavioral manifestation of developmental dyslexia differ between opaque English and transparent orthographies like Italian or Finnish?
- **Expected Answer:** While the underlying phonological processing deficit is shared across languages, children learning shallow/transparent orthographies (1:1 letter-sound mapping like Finnish or Italian) master single-letter decoding rules within months and eventually decode words *accurately*, manifesting dyslexia primarily through **persistent slow, dysfluent reading speed** (100–140 WPM). In deep, opaque English (which requires 2.5–3 years to master multiple grain sizes and irregular exceptions), dyslexia manifests primarily as **inaccurate word decoding** in addition to slow speed.
- **Reference:** Chapter 1, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 1.1: Orthographic Depth Across World Writing Systems and Cross-Linguistic Dyslexia*.

### Q03 (Oculomotor Psychophysics — Ch. 2)
- **Question:** State the empirical averages in skilled English reading for (a) fixation duration, (b) forward saccade length, (c) content-word vs. function-word fixation percentages, (d) regression rate, and (e) the asymmetric boundaries of the perceptual span (Rayner et al., 2016).
- **Expected Answer:** (a) Mean fixation duration is **225–250 ms**; (b) mean forward saccade length is **7–9 character spaces** (taking 20–35 ms); (c) readers fixate **~85% of content words** and **~35% of function words** (~65% of function words are skipped via parafoveal preview); (d) **10%–15% of saccades are regressions** (backward eye movements); and (e) the perceptual span extends **3–4 character spaces to the left** of fixation and **14–15 character spaces to the right** of fixation (never extending to lines above or below).
- **Reference:** Chapter 2, Sections *Quantitative Parameters of Skilled Eye Movements*, Table 2.1, and *The Gaze-Contingent Moving Window Paradigm*.

### Q04 (Cross-Script Oculomotor Reasoning — Ch. 2)
- **Question:** How do we know that the rightward asymmetry of the English perceptual span (14–15 characters right vs. 3–4 left) is driven by attentional reading direction rather than hardwired retinal anatomy, and how does the perceptual span change in Hebrew and Chinese?
- **Expected Answer:** When Pollatsek et al. (1981) tested bilingual Israelis reading right-to-left **Hebrew**, the perceptual span reversed direction inside the same readers' eyes, extending **14 characters to the left** and only 3–4 characters to the right. In logographic **Chinese** (where characters pack high semantic density into compact blocks), the perceptual span narrows to **1 character left and 2–3 characters right** (with 2–3 character saccades), yet total information intake per minute converges to the same ~250–350 English-word-equivalent ceiling.
- **Reference:** Chapter 2, Section *The Gaze-Contingent Moving Window Paradigm and the Asymmetric Perceptual Span* & Table 2.1.

### Q05 (Computational Models of Eye Control — Ch. 2)
- **Question:** How does the serial E-Z Reader model explain word skipping without allowing parallel multi-word lexical identification, and on what crucial practical constraint do E-Z Reader and the parallel SWIFT model completely agree?
- **Expected Answer:** E-Z Reader splits lexical access into a fast familiarity check ($L_1$) and full lexical access ($L_2$) while decoupling covert attention shifts from physical saccade programming: completion of $L_1$ on word $w_n$ starts programming the eye movement to $w_{n+1}$, while $L_2$ finishes rapidly and shifts covert attention to $w_{n+1}$ in the parafovea. If $w_{n+1}$ is short and predictable, its $L_1$ finishes during the cancelable labile phase of the motor program, canceling the saccade to $w_{n+1}$ and jumping directly to $w_{n+2}$. Both E-Z Reader and SWIFT agree that parafoveal preview is restricted to 1–2 adjacent words and shrinks whenever foveal word difficulty is high, ruling out whole-line speed reading.
- **Reference:** Chapter 2, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 2.1: E-Z Reader Versus SWIFT*.

### Q06 (Working Memory & Psycholinguistics — Ch. 3)
- **Question:** Why did Alan Baddeley (2000) add the "episodic buffer" to his working-memory model, why does Daneman & Carpenter's (1980) Reading Span Test out-predict passive digit span for reading comprehension, and how does Ericsson & Kintsch's (1995) Long-Term Working Memory (LTWM) resolve the interruption paradox?
- **Expected Answer:** Baddeley added the **episodic buffer** (~4 multi-modal chunks) to explain how readers bind phonological strings, visual layout, and long-term semantic schemas into coherent clauses (recalling 15–20 words in a sentence vs. 5–7 unrelated words). Daneman & Carpenter's Reading Span Test correlates strongly ($r \approx 0.50\text{–}0.72$) with comprehension because it measures simultaneous **processing plus storage** rather than passive storage. Ericsson & Kintsch's LTWM explains why an interrupted reader does not lose the plot after a 5-minute phone call: experts store rich discourse structures in long-term memory attached to compact **retrieval pointers** rapidly reactivated from short-term working memory.
- **Reference:** Chapter 3, Sections *Baddeley's Multi-Component Working Memory Architecture*, *Reading Span*, and *Long-Term Working Memory*.

### Q07 (Syntactic Complexity & Prosody — Ch. 3)
- **Question:** Using Gibson's Dependency Locality Theory (DLT) and Fodor's Implicit Prosody Hypothesis, explain why center-embedded object-relative clauses and unpunctuated garden-path sentences cause working-memory failure during silent reading.
- **Expected Answer:** Under Gibson's DLT, center-embedded clauses (*"The reporter that the senator that the prime minister appointed attacked..."*) maximize both **Storage Cost** (holding multiple unresolved syntactic heads open) and **Integration Cost** (long linear distance across multiple similar human nouns before verbs attach), causing similarity-based interference in working memory. Under Fodor's Implicit Prosody Hypothesis, silent readers project mental intonation and phrasing contours onto text in the phonological loop; ambiguous or missing punctuation triggers default prosodic grouping (Late Closure) that crashes when the disambiguating main verb arrives.
- **Reference:** Chapter 3, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 3.1: Syntactic Garden Paths, Dependency Locality, and Implicit Prosody in Silent Reading*.

### Q08 (Discourse Comprehension Levels — Ch. 4)
- **Question:** Define the three representational levels of Walter Kintsch's (1988) Construction-Integration (CI) model, compare their retention half-lives, and explain why a reader with an intact Propositional Textbase can still fail on transfer problems.
- **Expected Answer:** (1) **Surface Code:** verbatim wording and syntax, which decays within seconds to minutes. (2) **Propositional Textbase:** explicit semantic propositions (microstructure and macrostructure), which lasts hours to days and supports literal recall or summarization. (3) **Situation Model:** an integrated mental simulation combining the textbase with the reader's prior knowledge, spatial/causal models, and goals, enduring for months or years. A reader who builds only a Textbase can repeat what the author said verbally but cannot solve novel transfer or troubleshooting problems because the causal bridges to prior schemas were never integrated.
- **Reference:** Chapter 4, Section *The Three Levels of Text Representation* & Table 4.1.

### Q09 (Event Segmentation & Explanatory Depth — Ch. 4)
- **Question:** What happens to reading speed and working-memory object accessibility when a narrative crosses a temporal boundary like *"An hour later"* versus *"A moment later"* (Zwaan, 1996), and how do Rozenblit & Keil (2002) diagnose the Illusion of Explanatory Depth?
- **Expected Answer:** In Zwaan's (1996) Event-Indexing experiments, crossing an event boundary (*"An hour later"*) causes a spike in reading time on the boundary sentence (as the brain closes the old event frame and instantiates a new Situation Model) and sharply reduces working-memory accessibility of objects from the pre-shift scene (**the textual doorway effect**). Rozenblit & Keil (2002) showed that people overestimate their understanding of causal systems because they confuse lexical label familiarity with a runnable Situation Model; forcing a step-by-step causal trace and schematic drawing exposes the missing links.
- **Reference:** Chapter 4, Sections *Empirical Deep-Dive 4.1* and *Applied Cognitive Laboratory 4.2*.

### Q10 (Prior Knowledge vs. General Skill — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** Describe the $2 \times 2$ design and key result of Recht and Leslie's (1988) Baseball Study, and explain how McNamara et al.'s (1996) Coherence Reversal Effect proves that no single static text density is optimal for all readers.
- **Expected Answer:** Recht & Leslie (1988) crossed High vs. Low general reading ability with High vs. Low baseball knowledge ($N=64$ 7th/8th graders). On recall, physical model re-enactment, summarization, and importance sorting of a baseball passage, **Low Reading Ability + High Baseball Knowledge (LA/HK) students significantly outperformed High Reading Ability + Low Baseball Knowledge (HA/LK) students**. McNamara et al. (1996) subsequently proved the **Coherence Reversal Effect**: Low-Knowledge readers require maximally explicit, high-cohesion texts to build a situation model, whereas High-Knowledge readers learn *better* on transfer tests from denser, minimally cohesive texts that force them to actively retrieve their own schemas to bridge gaps.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Sections *The Classic "Baseball Study": Recht and Leslie (1988)* and *The Coherence Reversal Effect: McNamara et al. (1996)*.

### Q11 (Lexical Acquisition & The Matthew Effect — Ch. 5)
- **Question:** Why can direct classroom vocabulary lists account for only a tiny fraction of a student's annual vocabulary growth (Anderson & Nagy, 1992), and what quantitative exposure gap drives Stanovich's (1986) Matthew Effect?
- **Expected Answer:** Students learn ~3,000–4,000 new words per year between Grades 3 and 12, whereas classroom vocabulary lists teach at most 300–400 words per year; the remaining ~90% is acquired incrementally through wide independent reading via contextual vector narrowing ($5\%\text{–}15\%$ per single encounter) and morphological decomposition. Anderson, Wilson, & Fielding (1988) showed that a 90th-percentile fifth-grade reader reads **~1.8 to 2.3 million words/year** outside school compared to **<60,000 words/year** for a 10th-percentile reader—a 35-fold exposure gap.
- **Reference:** Chapter 5, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 5.1: The Matthew Effect in Vocabulary Acquisition and Morphological Bootstrapping*.

### Q12 (Refuting Speed Reading & RSVP — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** Using the findings of Rayner et al. (2016) and Schotter, Tran, & Rayner (2014), explain the three distinct neurobiological/cognitive reasons why (a) reading whole lines at once, (b) suppressing subvocalization, and (c) flashing words via RSVP at 600–1,000 WPM fail to preserve deep comprehension.
- **Expected Answer:** (a) **Retinal acuity and crowding:** High-acuity cone vision is restricted to the $2^\circ$ fovea (6–8 letters) and $5^\circ$ parafovea (14–15 chars); peripheral photoreceptors cannot resolve letters across whole lines. (b) **Phonological loop suppression:** Suppressing inner speech via articulatory tasks disables the phonological working-memory store and implicit prosody required to parse complex syntax and long-distance dependencies. (c) **Blocked regressions in RSVP:** Saccades take only ~10% of reading time (not 80%), and RSVP prevents the reader from pausing on dense words or executing the **10%–15% regressive saccades** required to resolve syntactic ambiguities (which Schotter et al., 2014 proved are causally necessary for comprehension).
- **Reference:** Chapter 6, Section *Why You Cannot "Read Whole Lines at a Glance" or "Eliminate Subvocalization"*.

### Q13 (First-Principles Calculation of the 500-WPM Ceiling — Ch. 6)
- **Question:** Derive the ~500 WPM physiological ceiling of full-text reading from the temporal latencies of visual transmission, lexical access, saccade programming, and saccade flight time.
- **Expected Answer:** Retina-to-VWFA visual latency takes $\sim 50\text{–}60\text{ ms}$, the $L_1$ lexical familiarity check takes $\sim 65\text{–}100\text{ ms}$, and overlapping oculomotor programming ($125\text{–}150\text{ ms}$) sets a minimum sustainable fixation duration of **$\sim 180\text{–}200\text{ ms}$** (mean $190\text{ ms}$) plus **$25\text{ ms}$** of saccadic flight time = **$215\text{ ms}$ per fixation cycle** ($\approx 4.65\text{ fixations/sec}$ or $279\text{ fixations/min}$). Because foveal/parafoveal acuity identifies at most **$1.5\text{ to }1.8\text{ words per fixation}$** (even with zero regressions), $279\text{ fixations/min} \times 1.8\text{ words/fixation} \approx \mathbf{502\text{ WPM}}$. Any rate above ~500–600 WPM mathematically requires skipping whole multi-word phrases (skimming).
- **Reference:** Chapter 6, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 6.1: Quantitative Anatomy of the 500-WPM Physiological Ceiling*.

### Q14 (Strategic Reading & Standards of Coherence — Ch. 7)
- **Question:** How do effective skimmers sample paragraphs under time constraints (Duggan & Payne, 2009), and how do van den Broek's "Standards of Coherence" and the Protégé Effect alter online inference generation?
- **Expected Answer:** Effective skimmers do not move their eyes faster over every word; they read titles, headings, captions, and the **first and last sentences of paragraphs** at normal foveal speed (~250–300 WPM) while skipping interior sentences to extract macrostructure at the cost of local caveats. Under van den Broek's model, readers studying for an exam or preparing to teach another person (**the Protégé Effect**, Fiorella & Mayer, 2013) adopt high **Standards of Coherence**, slowing down on causally dense sentences and doubling explanatory and predictive bridging inferences compared to entertainment browsing.
- **Reference:** Chapter 7, Sections *Distinguishing Five Reading Regimes* and *Empirical Deep-Dive 7.1*.

### Q15 (Cognitive Poetics & Narrative Simulation — Ch. 8)
- **Question:** According to Mar and Oatley (2008), Green and Brock (2000), and Miall and Kuiken (1994), what neural and cognitive processes occur during literary fiction reading that are destroyed when a novel is reduced to a plot summary?
- **Expected Answer:** Literary fiction operates as an embodied simulation of social intentionality (Mar & Oatley, 2008), recruiting the default-mode **mentalizing network** (mPFC, TPJ, pSTS) and sensorimotor cortex while inducing **narrative transportation** (Green & Brock, 2000) and **anomalous suspense** (Gerrig, 1993). Furthermore, **stylistic foregrounding / defamiliarization** (Miall & Kuiken, 1994) deliberately slows fixation times on striking metaphors or syntax to activate affective salience networks and prompt perspective shifts. Compressing a novel into a plot outline destroys temporal suspense, unreliable subtext, stylistic defamiliarization, and productive ambiguity.
- **Reference:** Chapter 8, Sections *Fiction as a Flight Simulator*, *Narrative Transportation*, and *Empirical Deep-Dive 8.1*.

### Q16 (Memory Consolidation & The Testing Effect — Ch. 9)
- **Question:** State the exact 5-minute vs. 1-week retention percentages and forgetting rates for the Repeated Study (`SSSS`) and Repeated Testing (`STTT`) groups in Roediger & Karpicke (2006), and explain why `SSSS` learners misjudged their own future retention.
- **Expected Answer:** At **5 minutes**, the `SSSS` group (averaging 14.2 passage readings) recalled **81%** of idea units compared to **70%** for the `STTT` group (who read during only 1 period—3.4 readings—and took 3 free-recall tests without feedback). However, at **1 week**, `SSSS` recall plunged to **42%** (**52% forgetting**), whereas `STTT` recall remained at **61%** (**only 13% forgetting**). `SSSS` learners confidently predicted they would remember more because repeated reading creates high **perceptual fluency** in the VWFA, tricking metacognitive monitoring into mistaking current reading ease for durable storage strength.
- **Reference:** Chapter 9, Section *The Testing Effect: Roediger and Karpicke (2006)* & Figure B.2.

### Q17 (Study Technique Utility & Note-Taking Qualification — Ch. 9)
- **Question:** Which two techniques received the "High Utility" rating in Dunlosky et al. (2013), which three received "Moderate Utility," and what crucial replication qualification must be included when citing Mueller & Oppenheimer's (2014) longhand vs. laptop note-taking study?
- **Expected Answer:** **High Utility:** Practice Testing (retrieval practice) and Distributed (spaced) Practice. **Moderate Utility:** Elaborative Interrogation, Self-Explanation, and Interleaved Practice (while Highlighting, Rereading, Summarization, Keyword Mnemonic, and Imagery received Low Utility). Regarding Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014): while laptop typists often transcribe verbatim (impairing conceptual application compared to slower, synthesizing longhand writers), replications (Morehead et al., 2019) prove the critical mechanism is **generative synthesis vs. verbatim transcription**—digital notes written closed-book in the reader's own words achieve equal conceptual mastery.
- **Reference:** Chapter 9, Sections *Evaluating Ten Study Techniques: Dunlosky et al. (2013)*, Table 9.1, and *The Note-Taking Paradox*.

### Q18 (Interpolated Retrieval / Forward Testing Effect — Ch. 9)
- **Question:** What is the "Forward Testing Effect" demonstrated by Szpunar, Khan, and Schacter (2013), and how does taking a recall quiz after Segments 1–3 improve learning of brand-new material in Segment 4?
- **Expected Answer:** In Szpunar et al. (2013), inserting brief 2-minute recall quizzes after Segments 1, 2, and 3 of a four-part sequence cut **mind-wandering during Segment 4 in half** ($19\%$ vs. $41\%$), prevented **proactive interference** from earlier segments via episodic context segregation, and increased test performance on the brand-new Segment 4 material by **more than 25%** compared to continuous study or reviewing summaries.
- **Reference:** Chapter 9, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 9.1: The Forward Testing Effect (Interpolated Retrieval)*.

### Q19 (Screen vs. Print Meta-Analysis — Ch. 10)
- **Question:** In Delgado et al.'s (2018) meta-analysis of 54 studies ($N=171,055$), what was the overall effect size of reading media on comprehension, which three moderator variables drove the screen deficit, and how did the effect change between 2000 and 2017?
- **Expected Answer:** Digital screens showed a significant comprehension deficit compared to print of **Hedges' $g = -0.21$**. The deficit was driven by three moderators: (1) **Text genre:** the deficit occurred specifically on **informational/expository texts** ($g = -0.25$), while pure **narrative fiction showed zero deficit** between screen and print; (2) **Time constraints:** time-constrained reading magnified the print advantage ($g = -0.26$) compared to self-paced reading; and (3) **Scrolling** impaired comprehension more than fixed pages. Counterintuitively, the print advantage **increased linearly from 2000 to 2017** despite higher screen resolution and digital familiarity.
- **Reference:** Chapter 10, Section *Paper Versus Pixel*.

### Q20 (Metacognitive Miscalibration & Attention Residue — Ch. 10)
- **Question:** How did Ackerman and Goldsmith (2011) explain poorer self-regulated learning on screens, and what did Leroy (2009) and Ward et al. (2017) discover about brief task interruptions and silent smartphones?
- **Expected Answer:** Ackerman & Goldsmith (2011) proved that screen readers exhibit **metacognitive overconfidence** in their Predictions of Performance (POP), causing them to terminate self-regulated study prematurely. Leroy (2009) showed that glancing at a secondary task leaves **attention residue** that degrades executive focus for 10–20 minutes after returning to the text, and Ward et al. (2017) proved that even a **silent, face-down smartphone on the desk** significantly reduces working-memory capacity (OSPAN) because the Central Executive expends tonic inhibition to suppress checking it.
- **Reference:** Chapter 10, Sections *Why Do Screens Impair Expository Reading?* and *Empirical Deep-Dive 10.1*.

### Q21 (Information Theory & Lossy Summarization Failure Modes — Ch. 11)
- **Question:** Why is compressing a 2,000-word chapter into a 300-word synthesis mathematically lossy under Shannon's Rate-Distortion Theory, and what five systematic failure modes occur in naive AI summarization?
- **Expected Answer:** Because a $6\times\text{–}8\times$ token reduction reduces mutual information ($I(X;\hat{X}) < H(X)$), non-zero distortion $D(X,\hat{X}) > 0$ is unavoidable outside pure verbatim repetition. The five systematic failure modes are: (1) **Qualification stripping (hedging collapse)**, turning bounded empirical findings into false absolutes; (2) **Causal chain flattening**, dropping intermediate steps and boundary exceptions; (3) **False confidence / fluency illusions**, masking desirable difficulties; (4) **Emphasis distortion / middle-of-context neglect**; and (5) **Voice, subtext, and aesthetic erasure** in literary narrative.
- **Reference:** Chapter 11, Sections *Information Theory and the Rate-Distortion Reality of Prose Compression* and *Five Systematic Failure Modes of Naive AI Summarization*.

### Q22 (Vector Retrieval Geometry & OmniTome Calibration — Ch. 11)
- **Question:** In the documented OmniTome / Smart Reader architecture using local 1024-dimensional `BGE-M3` embeddings, what quantitative improvement in retrieval separation was achieved by `[CLS]` pooling calibration, and why are dense bi-encoders vulnerable to negation blindness?
- **Expected Answer:** Switching `BGE-M3` from uncalibrated pooling to normalized **`[CLS]` pooling calibration** improved the retrieval-separation metric ($\Delta \text{sim} = \overline{\text{sim}}_{\text{pos}} - \overline{\text{sim}}_{\text{neg}}$) from **0.2520 to 0.4225** across normalized 1,200–2,400-word reading segments. Dense bi-encoders remain vulnerable to **negation blindness** because two sentences sharing 95% of their domain nouns and verbs with opposite truth values (*"requires neural input"* vs. *"does NOT require neural input"*) map to nearly identical points in $\mathbb{R}^{1024}$ ($\text{cosine similarity} > 0.94$), requiring exact source provenance verification.
- **Reference:** Chapter 11, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 11.1: Vector Embedding Geometry, Cosine Similarity Traps, and Calibrated Provenance Retrieval*.

### Q23 (Forensic Compression Audit — Ch. 11)
- **Question:** In Applied Cognitive Laboratory 11.2, what three specific epistemic errors occurred when the 165-word source passage on Herbst et al. (2023) feline purring was compressed into an unconstrained 38-word AI summary?
- **Expected Answer:** (1) **Preparation over-generalization:** converting sufficiency in *excised* larynges into the false claim that living cats never use neural signals or muscles to purr; (2) **Polarity inversion (negation drop):** fusing topical keywords (`25–30 Hz`, `bone healing`) with internet priors to invert *"no controlled clinical trial has directly demonstrated..."* into an affirmative claim; and (3) **Severed provenance:** omitting source links so the reader cannot detect the dropped caveats.
- **Reference:** Chapter 11, Section *Applied Cognitive Laboratory 11.2: A Side-by-Side Audit of Lossy Compression Versus Source Text*.

- **Expected Answer:** (A) **Unassisted Linear Reading (120 min at 250 WPM):** suffers ~35% middle-book mind-wandering and proactive interference, yielding **38%–45%** 7-day delayed recall. (B) **Lossy Summary-Only Reading (18 min on 4,800 synthesized words):** captures top-level gist rapidly, but scores **<45%–55%** on qualification, equation, and multi-hop ground-truth questions due to $6\times$ lossy compression. (C) **Two-Representation Integrated Protocol (~95 min total: 15 min schema priming + 60 min differential-depth source reading + 20 min interpolated retrieval commits):** saves 20% clock time over passive linear reading while nearly doubling 7-day situation-model retention and qualification accuracy.
- **Reference:** Chapter 12, Section *Empirical Deep-Dive 12.1: Designing the Two-Representation Workflow*.
